# The Game of Life: A Quest Guide

*An open-source field report. Version 1.0: deliberately crude, built to be forked.*

Mario Blandini

When I work with young people who get to know me, someone always asks: *How did you do it?*

Toured with a ballet company, served as a Marine, led in venture capital boardrooms, certified in Thai massage, and actively sounds Taps at graveside and civic ceremonies. Those worlds don't seem to go together, and they know I failed before succeeding. They want to know how I got through the hard times and still found success in the game of life.

Most of them assume the answer is brute force. Toughness. Grinding harder than everyone else. No pain, no gain.

It isn't. **The answer is choice, and urgency.**

**Choice** means that when life dragged me into a room I didn't pick, I went looking for a door I *could* pick.

**Urgency** means I didn't wait long to open it. I grieved, but I didn't let the pain sit and tear me down. I used it as fuel for something I chose. And every room I chose made the next one make more sense.

This field report unpacks how that works: turning pain into growth through crucibles. Thinking about the answer, I realized something bigger. Growth and a wider view of the world are the reward of reaching excellence through failure and repetition. But I've come to believe the best measure of a life well lived is something else:

> **A life well lived isn't measured by what happened to you. It's measured by the choices you make for your character in the game of life:** the doors you opened, the challenging rooms you chose to stay in, and the worldview lenses you chose to pick up.

That's the answer. This guide is how it works for me, so you can use it too. My full quest log is further down.

> **The short version:** Life is a quest game, and friction is built into the map. Some rooms you walk into. Some you get pushed into. Some have trap doors. The same friction that tears you down in a room you didn't pick can build you up in one you did. You don't control which doors slam in front of or behind you. You choose which door you open next, and whether you pick up what each room leaves you.

## You don't get to pick every room in the game

Some of the rooms you've been in, you never chose. Maybe a family came apart. Maybe someone got sick, a job vanished, or you're stuck somewhere you can't leave yet. Nobody asked you.

Say it out loud: **this room was forced on me.**

Now look around. Every room has more than one door. One of them leads somewhere you've always wanted to go. That door is yours.

## How the game works

### The map

- **Rooms** are crucibles. Each one has real pressure, a long grind, and a permanent change in your character when you walk out.
- **Doors** are choices.
- **Hallways** are the space between rooms, where you rest, recover, and figure out what's next.
- **Safe rooms** are save points: sleep, family, friends, faith, a steady routine. They're part of the map, not a detour from it.

### Solo rooms and group rooms

Some rooms you clear alone: a fitness standard, perfecting a language, a season of grief. Others you can only clear with a team or a guild: a performing ensemble, a boot camp platoon, a founding team.

Group rooms break down your ego to build something bigger than you. Solo rooms teach you to show up when nobody's watching. A strong character in the game needs both.

### Some rooms you choose. Some choose you.

- **Involuntary rooms** drop on you: loss, illness, a breakup, a layoff.
- **Voluntary rooms** are the ones you walk into on purpose.
- **Most big rooms are both.** You choose the room, but not everything it throws at you.

### Every room has a card

You score any room on two stats, each with three settings:

| Stat | Settings |
| --- | --- |
| **Weight** | Light · Heavy · Max |
| **Duration** | Sprint (weeks to months) · Campaign (a season to a couple of years) · Saga (many years, or a lifetime) |

Three examples:

- **Becoming fluent in another language:** Light weight, Campaign duration.
- **Military and first responder training:** Max weight, Sprint duration.
- **Freemasonry:** Heavy weight, Saga duration.

The card scores the room. It doesn't tell you what the room was worth. That's what lenses are for. And nobody else gets to rate your rooms. Only you know what a room weighed, and what it cost you.

### Lenses: how your character levels up

Rooms don't have levels. Your character does, and only when it's earned.

A lens is a way of seeing the world. Every room can hand you a new one or sharpen one you already carry. I believe there are five types, each with its own territory:

| Lens | Its territory |
| --- | --- |
| **Grit** | Body and endurance: what you can withstand, and how to keep going |
| **Craft** | Skill and creation: how mastery is built, and how new things get made |
| **Bonds** | People: teamwork, leadership, empathy, and your own heart |
| **Wisdom** | The mind: why things are the way they are and how to think about them |
| **Spirit** | Meaning and morals: what's right, what it costs, and what lasts beyond you |

Any room can hand you any lens. There's Spirit in a marathon, and Bonds on a solo trail.

### The debrief: your level-up capstone

When you clear a room in the game, before you open the next door, debrief. Ask yourself:

- Which lens did this room give me, or sharpen?
- How will I use it?

Name one primary lens, maybe a second. That's the level-up. Not the room itself: the capstone. The hardship happens to you. The reflection is a choice. **Hardship without chosen reflection is loot left on the floor.**

Sometimes a lens doesn't drop until several rooms later. You debrief, see nothing yet, and years afterward another room makes it clear. That's not a failed debrief. It's a delayed drop. Keep the room in your log and come back to it.

### Lens synergy: new rooms re-light old ones

Your quest log isn't fixed. Every new lens lets you re-read the rooms you've already cleared.

Say primary level-up for youth scouting is Grit. Years later, rooms that sharpen your Bonds and Wisdom lenses let you look back at scouting and see more: why the troop worked, what the breaking down was for. The old room didn't change. You did, so it's worth more now.

That's why rooms that seem unrelated matter. Ten rooms of the same kind sharpen one lens. Ten different rooms give you lenses that light each other up.

### Compounding: old level-ups pay forward

Synergy works forward too. The lenses you earn in hard rooms make the next room easier to enter and easier to clear. That's why many players find the later epochs go better than the early ones. It isn't luck. It's earlier level-ups paying out.

I saw it in my own log. After drum corps and the Marines, I went to college and earned a 4.0. Not because I was smarter than everyone else. Because after those rooms, college was easier.

People my age like to say, *I wish I knew then what I know now.* It doesn't work that way. What I know now couldn't have been handed to me. It came from time in rooms, and from rooms cleared one after another, each one paying into the next.

That's the reward behind the reward. The work isn't just toil you survive. It's an investment that keeps paying. **So choose your next room with hope, not dread.** You're not just enduring it. You're building the player who'll clear the one after.

There's no climbing. An 18-year-old can walk into a Max room, and a 50-year-old into a Light one. Progress isn't how big your rooms get. It's how many lenses you carry, and how clearly you see through them.

### Failure is a feature

Most runs at hard things fail. That's not a bug. It's how excellence works. Every elite player you admire in the game of life failed rooms over and over before clearing them.

A failed run still earns XP (experience points). It shows you exactly what your character can't do yet. Respawn, adjust, run it again or return to it later.

### The game runs across every epoch of life

**Youth** (under 18) · **Young adult** (18–25) · **Adult** (26–45) · **Midlife** (46–65) · **Retirement** (66+)

Involuntary rooms show up in every epoch. Some epochs leave you no space to choose a voluntary one, and that's okay. Most doors wait for you. A few lock or unlock after a certain epoch, though life offers more rooms than any single player can explore.

And doors from an earlier epoch can be reopened. Gamers call it **New Game+**: replaying with everything you've carried since. Comebacks count, and they're often the easiest place to start.

You don't beat this game. You explore it.

## The hidden mechanic

Friction and failure are built into reality. In a room you didn't choose, they tear you down. In a room you did choose, the same forces build you up. It's not a cheat. It's a mechanic the game has always had, and many players never learn to use it.

When your conditions allow it, here's how:

> When life pushes you into a room you didn't pick, **grieve it.** Work through it, with people, not alone. Then, when you're ready, and before you talk yourself out of it, **open a door you did pick.**

**Why it works:** an involuntary room takes control away from you. A voluntary one hands some back. You can't undo the first. You can choose what you build next.

**One exception.** If your room involves abuse, violence, or thoughts of hurting yourself, your first move is not a crucible. It's telling a loved one, trusted adult, counselor, or doctor. Good players don't solo a raid that needs a healer.

## When rooms go wrong

### The trap door

Sometimes a room you chose changes its rules mid-grind: an injury, a market collapse, a toxic leader. It becomes an involuntary room with you already inside.

**You chose the door. You didn't choose the trap.** Don't carry blame for it. Treat it like any room you didn't pick: grieve it, get help, find the exit.

### The double raid

Life rarely hands you one room at a time. An involuntary room can drop on you while you're mid-grind in one you chose.

Your quest log can hold more than one quest line, but your strength has limits. Pausing or leaving a chosen room to handle one you didn't choose is a legitimate move. It's not quitting. Most doors will still be there.

### The hallway

After a heavy room, you may not have the strength to open another door. That's normal.

The hallway is part of the map. It's where grieving happens, and where you rest, recover, and reflect. Time there isn't stalling. Players who skip the hallway burn out in the next room and/or leave loot on the floor.

## Before you see my quest log: this isn't a high score

Stacking hard rooms to flex doesn't count. Stacking them to outrun your pain doesn't count either. That's just running from the boss.

That doesn't mean you can't be proud. Pride in rooms you earned isn't a flex. A flex is about being seen. Pride is about what you carried out, and it gives you the standing to help the next player through.

After you leave a room, ask yourself:

- Did I gain a new lens, or sharpen one I had?
- Am I more patient with people?
- Do I handle a bad day better than I used to?

If the answers are no, you may have been grinding for points. And don't score yourself alone. Ask someone who knows you well. They'll see what you can't.

I'm GenX, so I am in the later half of my quest. Here's the log behind "how did you do it": my main rooms, grouped by epoch, plus a few examples of side quests. They're in the order I entered them, not ranked. Plenty of them overlapped. I've carried more than one quest line at a time more often than not.

**Youth**

- **Childhood trauma** · *involuntary* — Growing up adapting to an abusive home.
- **Drum Corps performer** · *voluntary* — 12-hour rehearsal days. Trading ego for ensemble.

**Young adult**

- **United States Marine** · *voluntary* — Six years giving up civilian individuality for discipline and endurance.
- **Divorce #1** · *involuntary* — Rebuilding identity, trust, and stability.

**Adult**

- **Global technologist and industry spokesperson** · *voluntary* — Holding rooms of experts on a global stage.
- **Divorce #2** · *involuntary* — A deeper rebuild, from zero.
- **Cancer caregiving** · *both* — Five years beside my mother, from her diagnosis at 53 to her death at 58.

**Midlife**

- **VC-backed startup CMO** · *voluntary* — Growth targets under investor pressure, more than once.
- **Job loss** · *involuntary* — Nine months waiting tables before a career pivot.
- **100 services sounding live Taps** · *voluntary, lifelong* — Zero-mistake moments for families at the graveside.
- **1st class Marine fitness test at age 17–20 standard** · *voluntary, New Game+, in progress* — Holding a GenX body to a young adult's bar.
- **Brass performance after 36 years away** · *voluntary, New Game+, in progress* — Rebuilding a low-brass embouchure and regaining excellence.
- **Freemasonry and Knights Templar** · *voluntary, lifelong* — Moral and spiritual work that's never finished.

**Retirement**

- TBD, but I have a lot of ideas beyond New Game+ crucibles.

**Side quests: smaller rooms, same lesson**

- Toured with a ballet company, in youth.
- Mastered Spanish at a business level, for conferences across Latin America, as an adult.
- Certified as a Thai massage therapist, in midlife.

The log isn't tidy, and I won't pretend it is:

- **Drum corps and the Marines** were chosen, but I was partly running from home. I didn't see it at the time. By my own rule, those two don't score. They stay in the log because their lenses dropped years later, and they're a big reason I could clear the rooms I didn't choose.
- **The second divorce and my mother's illness** landed together, with no chosen room or hallway in between.
- **Caregiving** broke my own categories. I didn't choose my mother's illness. I chose to stay.

What you see now is my late game. The early game went back and forth for years and didn't feel like success at the time. The level-ups I earned then are why it appears to the casual observer that my chosen crucibles were easy for me.

I'm proud of the season of life I'm in, and I won't hide it. Not so you'll compare. Because the reward on the other side of a chosen crucible is real, and many people never get to experience what it feels like or see how the effort paid off.

You won't play this game the way I did, and you shouldn't try. I am flattered when someone calls me "Super Mario". I respond that I am not special, and that the real game of life is about working the right way to develop one's superpower(s). I believe all humans have them, and that crucibles develop them, but only if you seek the room and knock on the door.

## How to choose your next door

1. **Start from your bucket list.** Pick a challenge that you want to do, not one that would impress people. If you'd want it even if nobody ever found out, it's the right kind of door. Most bucket lists hold two kinds of doors: new experiences, and comebacks. A comeback is often the easiest next door, especially when you're answering a room you didn't choose, because you already know the way in.
2. **Find a guild or tribe.** When you can, pick a group room: a team, an ensemble, a crew, a coach. The guild carries you on the days you can't carry yourself, and you get to help carry others.
3. **Start where failing is cheap.** Fear of failing in public stops more players than difficulty does. At a military funeral, the bugler often stands out of sight. The family hears the call, not the person. Start somewhere like that.
4. **Name the lenses you want.** What do you hope to see differently when you clear the room? Decide before you go in, so you'll know if you got it.
5. **Check the epoch.** Most doors wait. A few lock with time. Know which is which.
6. **Grieve first, but don't wait for perfect.** There's no perfect moment. There's ready enough, and urgency goes hand in hand with choice.

## How to leave a room

- **Clear it:** you got the lens you came for.
- **Pause it:** a double raid needs your strength somewhere else.
- **Leave it:** it became a trap door, or it was never your room.

Leaving honestly isn't failing. Staying in a room that's breaking you, just for the scoreboard, is. XP is still earned.

## Quest menu

This list is incomplete and in no particular order, and is just to get you thinking. Pick crucibles from your bucket list, not only from this list.

- Run your first half or full marathon

## Version 1.0: fork this guide

This is version 1.0 of what I've seen: a flicker of light, caught after tuning my lenses through many years and many rooms. I'm simply a field operator reporting what I saw from my POV and how I came to see it, not an expert in any of the fields this touches.

The scoring and methods here are crude on purpose. They aren't science. They're bare architecture, built just well enough for a community to take over. That's where you come in. Copy this, change it, make it yours. The parts that most need builders:

- **Lens guides:** what each lens covers, and how to spot it in a debrief. Psychologists, coaches, chaplains, and teachers will see what I can't.
- **The quest menu:** a much bigger list of life's crucibles, with honest notes on cost, access, and which doors lock.
- **Calibration models:** which rooms deserve Max weight, and why. Argue it out.
- **Community scoring:** how players rate rooms together, so one person's Heavy and another's Max can be compared.
- **Much more:** things like maintaining and evolving the open source tradecraft above, game support networks for players working for a higher cause, and more.

If you're a player, not a builder, that counts too. Map your own rooms, and feed back into the main so the tradecraft works better for those that follow you.

This isn't therapy, and it isn't a program. It's simply a way of thinking, shared freely so it can spread wherever it helps.

**In the comments:** post the crucible room you're in right now. Just the room. No names, no details. That's enough.

> You don't control which doors slam behind you. But a life well lived is measured by what you choose: the next door, the rooms you stay in, and what you carry out of each one.

## The bigger game

Everything above is the single-player mode: one character, one quest log.

But nobody plays alone. Every door on this map was built by somebody: a corps, a unit, a lodge, a town. Some are being kept open. Some are quietly closing in the 21st century. And what you build here, every room you map and every lens you name, feeds the questions the bigger game has to answer.

That's the multiplayer layer. It's being developed right now, and you're invited.

## About the author

Mario has spent his life moving between worlds that rarely talk to each other. He marched brass with the Santa Clara Vanguard in 1989 and 1990, then traded the field for six years in the United States Marine Corps. He went on to spend three decades in technology, 20 of those years leading marketing organizations.

None of that made him an authority on difficulty. It gave him a vantage point. Having passed through more than one crucible and having lived and worked among people who never had the chance to, he began to notice a pattern that is hard to see from inside any single world.

Today he sounds live Taps as a volunteer bugler with many organizations, including the American Legion and Bugles Across America, with more than 100 graveside missions completed. He is one of the shrinking number of buglers still answering that call. He offers this guide not as a verdict, but as an observation from someone who happened to be standing where the light flickered.
